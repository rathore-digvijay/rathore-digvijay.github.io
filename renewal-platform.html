Context and my role

Renewals are the highest-margin, most time-sensitive flow in insurance distribution: a policy has an expiry date, and every day past it is lost revenue and a lapsed customer. The journey spans insurer systems, payment gateways, three communication channels and a calling floor, and every step of it can fail independently.

I own the architecture and technical roadmap for the platform end to end, and lead the engineers building it. Most of the design work is about failure: what happens when an insurer API times out mid-payment, when a nudge is delivered twice, when a lead is created from two sources at once.

Decisions and trade-offs

Event-driven, with idempotency as a hard rule

Nudges, payment callbacks and booking events all arrive more than once in practice. Every consumer is idempotent, with retries and dead-letter queues on RabbitMQ so a bad insurer response parks the message instead of losing the lead. The trade-off is operational surface — queues to watch, DLQs to drain — which is why the ops dashboard exposes retry directly rather than routing through engineering.

Single-click renewal over a redesigned form

Rather than shorten the renewal form, we removed it for eligible policies: pre-fetched quote, pre-validated data, one payment link. That alone added about 5% lead conversion, and the wider journey redesign plus targeted multi-channel nudges took renewal conversion up around 20%.

Protecting insurer integrations at the edge

Insurer APIs are the least elastic part of the system. Rate limiting and distributed caching sit in front of them so a traffic spike on our side does not become an outage on theirs. HPA tuning, runtime upgrades and cross-AZ transfer optimization cut AWS cost about 10% along the way.

Data modelling for volume, not for convenience

High-volume lifecycle data lives in MongoDB with deliberate schema design, indexing, sharding and replication, and Redis caching on hot paths. A Python renewal-notice PDF parser auto-tags policy documents at roughly 80% accuracy, removing a large manual step in lead preparation.

Team and how I led it

The platform is built by the larger half of my group, working with Product, Business, QA and DevOps. I run design reviews before implementation and hold the line on scalability when a commercially attractive shortcut would not survive volume — pushing back on non-scalable decisions is part of the job description here.

Alongside delivery I own hiring, reviews and promotions for the group: 5 engineers hired, 45+ interview panels run, several mentees promoted.
